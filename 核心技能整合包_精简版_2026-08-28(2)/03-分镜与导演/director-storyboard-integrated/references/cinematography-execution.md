# Cinematography Execution

Use this reference when a task needs professional camera execution: camera position, focal length, perspective, depth of field, focus, movement device, axis, dialogue coverage, or on-site lighting tools. Keep the output shootable: every technical choice must change perspective, information, emotion, or production feasibility.

## 1. Five-Director Camera And Movement Grammar

### Hitchcock: camera position creates information gap

- Core rule: camera position is viewpoint; viewpoint controls what the audience knows before, after, or instead of the character.
- Camera positions: voyeur POV through keyholes, door gaps, windows, curtains, blinds; high-angle vulnerability; low-angle threat; frame-restricted people trapped by doors, rails, corridors, stairwells; object-centered framing where a key, money, glass, knife, phone, or package becomes more important than the people.
- Movement: extremely slow push-in toward an object or face; dolly zoom only for violent subjective collapse; object tracking that keeps the suspense carrier centered; POV movement toward a threshold; locked-off observation when waiting itself is the threat.
- Shot size: reaction shots often matter more than action shots; close-ups of eyes, hands, and objects are emotional amplifiers; medium shots keep dialogue spatially grounded; wide shots establish the trap before tightening.
- Lens grammar: `35-50mm` for human observation and voyeur realism; `85-135mm` for compression, entrapment, and object pressure; `24-28mm` for subjective distortion, stairs, vertigo, or warped space.
- Failure boundary: do not use voyeur angles, slow pushes, or dolly zoom as decoration. Each must answer what information is hidden, who is watched, and what the audience knows that the character does not.

### Lynch: wrong movement means wrong reality

- Core rule: the camera should first make ordinary reality feel stable, then make it subtly wrong.
- Camera positions: over-perfect symmetry; low-angle views of ordinary ceilings, lamps, corridors, furniture, and empty rooms; empty-frame gazes that wait too long; strange subject placement where a character is too centered, too far to one side, or too small in a normal room.
- Movement: dreamlike slow push; movement that is too smooth for the situation; sudden jump-cut interruption; low-angle follow that makes everyday space monumental; slow threshold approach with sound arriving before proof.
- Shot size: hold empty rooms, chairs, hallway ends, curtains, faces listening to nothing; let normal objects become ritualistic through duration and placement.
- Lens grammar: `18-28mm` for uncanny distortion and wrong domestic space; `35-50mm` for normal baseline before drift; `85-200mm` for voyeur distance, inaccessible faces, and dream surveillance.
- Failure boundary: weirdness needs a normal baseline. Random distortion, random colors, and unreadable geography become noise instead of subconscious fear.

### Fincher: camera as system eye

- Core rule: the camera should feel planned, patient, precise, and stronger than the person.
- Camera positions: slight surveillance high angle; geometric symmetry; tiny human figure inside architecture; frame-within-frame using doors, screens, glass, cubicles, files, monitors; objective eye-level observation that refuses emotional comfort.
- Movement: mechanical smooth push; controlled follow; slow scan across evidence; aerial or god view for system pressure; locked-off frame for institutional control; screen/file insert as proof plane.
- Shot size: medium shots observe behavior; evidence close-ups must be legible; wide shots show the room, institution, furniture, or screen grid overpowering the person.
- Lens grammar: `35-50mm` for exact procedural observation; `24-32mm` for architecture and system geometry; `85-135mm` for person/detail compression, surveillance, and evidence pressure.
- Failure boundary: handheld chaos, decorative symmetry, and dark images without readable evidence break the system-eye grammar.

### Nolan: camera as architecture, time, and rule

- Core rule: clarity before complexity. A rule must become physical, spatial, or time-based before escalation.
- Camera positions: clear geography first; wide architectural frame with a human anchor; scale shot followed by face, hand, object, or clock; physical threshold in frame; IMAX-like detail where environment texture and human consequence coexist.
- Movement: motivated movement through space; practical physical motion; cross-cut clocks; repeated visual motif; camera moves that explain a rule, not just decorate scale.
- Shot size: start with readable geography, then cut to human/object proof; large-scale spectacle must return to a choice, body, or object.
- Lens grammar: maintain focal consistency and physical realism; use wide architectural lenses for scale, normal lenses for rule clarity, longer lenses for human consequence.
- Failure boundary: no complex cross-cutting before rules are taught. Spectacle without human cost, focal inconsistency, and unmotivated camera tricks make the impossible feel weightless.

### Tarantino: camera as stage, drama, and rupture

- Core rule: dialogue is blocking, posture, hands, objects, silence, and delayed explosion.
- Camera positions: establish room geography before close-ups; low-angle standoff; table-level object angle; trunk/box/object POV; camera watching hands, drinks, cigarettes, shoes, weapons, or etiquette violations.
- Movement: lateral move across faces and hands; dialogue hold that lets pressure ripen; sudden rupture after a long delay; push-in only when a line, gesture, or object shifts power.
- Shot size: wide/two-shot for geography and power distance; medium close-ups for listening and suspicion; inserts only when a prop can trigger reversal.
- Lens grammar: normal to short telephoto for theatrical confrontation; wider lenses for staged room geography; low-angle lenses for iconic threat.
- Failure boundary: close-up ping-pong kills room tension. Dialogue without hidden power shift is just talking; props without trigger function are decoration.

## 2. Focal Length And Perspective System

Focal length is not just "zoom". It controls perspective, spatial pressure, psychological distance, and how much the viewer feels inside or outside the event.

| Focal length | Perspective effect | Psychological use | Failure boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| `14mm` | extreme wide, heavy edge distortion, space stretches and bends | panic, loss of control, cramped surreal space, body/world distortion | becomes gimmicky if every room bends for no subjective reason |
| `24mm` | wide environment, strong depth, mild distortion near edges | exploration, corridor pressure, room geography, subtle unease | faces near edge deform; avoid for flattering emotion unless distortion is intended |
| `35mm` | human documentary width, person plus environment | grounded realism, walking with character, domestic tension, two-person blocking | can feel ordinary if composition has no visual owner |
| `50mm` | natural eye-level perspective, neutral compression | objective observation, restrained dialogue, everyday truth | can become bland if shot size, blocking, and light have no tension |
| `85mm` | portrait compression, separated background | emotional close-up, compressed pressure, private thought, object proof | overuse makes every shot shallow and pretty but spatially weak |
| `135mm+` | strong compression, voyeur distance, flattened depth | surveillance, isolation, unreachable person, crowd pressure | loses geography; must be paired with an established space |

Practical rules:

- Wide lenses increase spatial participation; long lenses increase distance, pressure, and observation.
- A focal change must answer: are we inside the character's body, observing them, spying on them, or trapping them against the background?
- Do not pick a focal length because it is "cinematic". State the perspective effect and emotional reason.
- In dialogue, focal length also decides honesty: `35-50mm` feels present; `85mm+` feels private, pressured, or watched.
- Establish space before using long-lens compression, otherwise the viewer cannot feel what has been compressed.

## 3. Depth Of Field And Focus System

Depth of field decides what information the viewer is allowed to read at the same time.

### Shallow depth of field

- Parameter range: approximately `f/1.4-f/2.8` or equivalent T-stop.
- Use for: emotion isolation, dream, memory, selective attention, private fear, a clue that separates from the world.
- Visual result: background melts, attention narrows, the viewer feels locked to a face, hand, text, flame, or object.
- Failure boundary: if every shot is shallow, the film loses space, tension, and staging. It becomes pretty blur instead of information control.

### Deep focus

- Parameter range: approximately `f/8-f/16` when light level allows.
- Use for: space horror, background threat, multi-line blocking, surveillance, room logic, viewers actively searching the frame.
- Visual result: foreground, midground, and background remain readable; danger can sit far behind the subject without a cut.
- Failure boundary: if everything is equally readable but the frame has no hierarchy, the viewer does not know where to look.

### T-stop, f-stop, and exposure logic

- `f-stop` describes aperture geometry; `T-stop` describes actual light transmission. Film production often thinks in T-stops for exposure consistency.
- Opening aperture affects exposure and depth of field at the same time. If a scene needs deep focus, the lighting plan must supply enough exposure.
- Focus, aperture, and light must be planned together; do not request deep focus in a dark room without a way to light it.

### Rack focus and focus behavior

- Rack focus must have a story route: focus starts on one information carrier, delays, then shifts to the proof or threat.
- Speed matters: slow rack = realization or dread; fast rack = surprise or urgent discovery; breathing focus = subjective instability.
- Focus drift can express exhaustion, hallucination, or dissociation, but must not be confused with sloppy focus.
- Focus breathing should be considered when a rack changes frame size; use it deliberately for unease or avoid it for clean procedural scenes.

Required focus wording:

```text
focus starts on -> why it delays -> rack/drift speed -> lands on -> what information changes
```

## 4. Movement Device Psychology

Choose the device because it changes how the viewer inhabits the scene.

| Device | Psychological meaning | Best use | Failure boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| handheld | human instability, urgency, body proximity | panic, pursuit, argument, documentary pressure | cheap if used before geography and blocking are clear |
| gimbal / steadicam | smooth subjective travel, guided exploration | corridors, dream walking, following a character through a live space | too smooth can feel like a showroom unless the route has micro-beats |
| dolly / track | controlled pressure, fate, precision | slow push, pull-back, evidence approach, power shift | dead if the move only says "cinematic" and reveals nothing |
| jib / crane | vertical revelation, power, loneliness, scale | reveal room layout, descend into trap, lift from person to system | overblown if scale does not change story meaning |
| slider | small controlled parallax | object reveal, table tension, hiding/revealing a face behind foreground | weak if used as decorative side motion |
| drone / aerial | god view, map, exposure, helplessness | landscape, institutional scale, layout proof, pursuit geography | can detach emotion if no human anchor follows |
| locked-off tripod | evidence, deadpan control, institutional pressure, waiting | empty room, surveillance, aftermath, character entering frame | static shots need internal change or short duration in AIGC formats |
| CCTV / fixed camera | system observation, dehumanization, proof | hospitals, offices, police, elevators, stores, corridors | loses emotion if used without contrast to human shots |

Movement must state:

```text
device -> start height/angle -> route -> speed -> end frame -> what new information appears
```

### Unified Camera / Edit / Transition Operator Grammar

Do not learn named moves as isolated tricks. Normalize every single or compound shot through one grammar:

```text
dramatic job
-> viewer relation
-> primary spatial operator
-> secondary orientation operator
-> optical or focus operator
-> edit/time operator when needed
-> transition coupling
-> landing frame and information change
```

Operator families:

| Family | Operators | What must be specified |
|---|---|---|
| camera state | locked, handheld, stabilized, body-mounted, vehicle-mounted | stability, height, angle, distance, permitted reaction |
| spatial translation | push/dolly in, pull/dolly out, truck, track, lead, follow, parallel move, pedestal, boom, crane, arc/orbit | axis, route, speed curve, subject-distance behavior, start/end frame |
| orientation | pan, tilt, roll, reframe, head-turn correction | pivot, direction, angular amount, trigger, new field revealed |
| subject relation | POV, overtake, counter-move, fixed pass-by, object tracking, reaction-first | who owns motion, who owns gaze, whether geography stays readable |
| optical/focus | zoom, crash zoom, inverse zoom, rack focus, focus drift, aperture/depth change | camera position, focal direction, subject-size change, focus start/landing |
| edit/time | hard cut, flash cut/frame intrusion, flashback entry/return, match cut, montage, speed change, freeze | trigger, source image, information delta, duration class, return or landing rule |
| transition coupling | occlusion wipe, foreground wipe, body wipe, whip-pan cut, light/blackout wipe, shape/motion match, sound prelap/tail bridge | covering surface or vector, hidden edit point, direction continuity, first revealed proof |

Compound-move construction rules:

1. Assign one primary spatial operator. It owns the viewer's changing position.
2. Add at most one secondary orientation operator to preserve, delay or redirect attention.
3. Add an optical or focus operator only when perspective, scale or information permission must change independently of camera position.
4. Couple a transition at the move's threshold or landing; do not let the transition become a second unrelated camera path.
5. Preserve at least one continuity invariant across a transition: subject screen position, motion vector, shape, foreground cover, light source, gaze target or sound tail.
6. In a 2-4 second internal AIGC beat, two independent primary camera paths are a split signal. Keep one dominant path and express the rest as subject motion, reframe, focus or an edit unless a verified long-take design requires more. A 4-30 second user-visible segment may contain several such sequential beats.

Reusable compound templates:

| Compound grammar | Execution chain | Dramatic use | Failure boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| push + rack focus | controlled axial push -> foreground information held -> focus transfers to background proof -> camera lands after proof becomes readable | realization, hidden watcher, clue handoff | focus and push cannot reveal unrelated facts at the same instant |
| pullback + spatial reveal | begin tight on face/object -> pull along a readable axis -> environment or second character enters by parallax -> land on changed power map | isolation becoming exposure, trap reveal | the reveal must alter story knowledge, not merely show a larger room |
| lateral track + pan correction | camera trucks parallel -> pan compensates to hold subject -> foreground repeatedly occludes -> correction stops on new obstacle or observer | pursuit, surveillance, live-space dialogue | uncontrolled correction feels like vague orbiting and loses screen direction |
| lead-to-follow handoff | camera leads subject backward -> subject crosses or passes lens -> camera pivots/repositions into follow -> route continues in the same screen direction | threshold crossing, confidence becoming pursuit | crossing must be visible; a hidden axis flip confuses geography |
| arc/orbit reveal | arc around subject while radius stays motivated -> foreground/background relationship changes -> hidden person, exit or object enters -> settle when information owner changes | social power shift, encirclement, relationship reveal | continuous circling without a reveal is decorative showroom motion |
| orbit transition | orbit begins in source scene -> body/foreground object fills frame -> hidden cut or material mutation -> orbit continues in the same angular direction and subject screen position -> cover clears onto new scene -> sound tail bridges | time/space transition, identity or wardrobe change | new scene must inherit direction, radius impression and landing composition; otherwise it reads as an arbitrary wipe |
| crane/boom + push | vertical move first establishes hierarchy or map -> axial push selects the human/object consequence -> land at eye or object level | system-to-person pressure, descending into a trap | scale reveal and emotional selection need separate readable phases |
| whip pan + matched cut | gaze/body trigger -> rapid pan creates directional blur -> cut inside blur -> new shot continues vector and approximate speed -> hard landing on proof/reaction | abrupt discovery, pursuit handoff, location jump | direction or speed mismatch produces a random transition rather than continuous force |
| fixed pass-by + body wipe | locked frame establishes geography -> subject crosses close and covers lens -> cut/change occurs under cover -> next subject edge clears with the same direction -> environment difference appears | efficient scene/time change, chase coverage | cover must genuinely fill the relevant frame area and preserve motion vector |
| axial dolly + inverse zoom | camera translates along lens axis while focal length changes in the opposite direction -> subject size stays nearly stable -> background perspective expands or compresses -> body reaction confirms perception change | violent recognition, vertigo, target becoming unreachable | ordinary push, zoom-only or digital scale change is not a dolly zoom |
| locked camera + crash zoom | camera position stays fixed -> sudden optical zoom isolates a face/object -> cut or reaction follows | aggressive attention seizure, clue punctuation, comic or thriller rupture | repeated use becomes editorial shouting; it needs a specific trigger |
| flash cut/frame intrusion | stable scene establishes baseline -> trigger sound/object/gaze -> one or a few brief alien images intrude -> stable scene returns -> body, sound or object residue proves the intrusion mattered | suppressed image, threat contamination, fractured perception | random shocking inserts without recurrence or consequence are noise; create inserts as separate edit plates when the video model cannot hold frame precision |
| flashback entry/return | present trigger -> matched action/object/sound/light interface -> past layer with a controlled difference -> decisive memory fact -> return trigger -> present action or residue changes | memory pressure, withheld causality, unreliable recollection | color grading alone cannot define time; entry, return and changed present behavior must be readable |
| match move / match shape cut | source action or shape approaches a precise state -> cut at peak similarity -> target action or shape continues -> difference becomes readable after continuity is accepted | compression of time, parallel action, associative transition | surface similarity without dramatic relation becomes an editing exercise |

Architecture rule: do not create a new runtime route for every named move or combination. Add a new entry only when it contributes a non-obvious physical constraint, information pattern, sound relation, transition invariant or failure boundary that cannot already be expressed by the operator grammar.

### Threshold State Machine

A door, hatch, curtain, stair landing, corridor bend or light boundary is not suspenseful merely because it exists. Track it as a state machine:

```text
boundary identity
/ current state: closed / ajar / open / locked / blocked / breached / self-closing
/ access owner
/ knowledge owner
/ visible slice and audible slice
/ crossing trigger
/ physical consequences
/ permission change after crossing
/ tail state
```

Every threshold change should alter at least one permission:

- visual permission: how much of the next space can be seen.
- acoustic permission: which room tone, voice or mechanical source can cross.
- physical permission: who can enter, leave, block, pursue, hide or carry an object through.
- social/system permission: who owns the key, code, invitation, uniform, command or right to open.
- identity/version permission: whether the person, object count, owner mark or room checksum still belongs to the same state after crossing.

Opening must change air, light, sound, shadow, nearby loose material and body posture. Closing must state whether it protects, traps, separates, silences, delays or transfers control. A half-open door is useful only when the visible and audible slices create a specific information inequality.

Failure boundary:

- Repeating door openings without changing access, information or power is decorative blocking.
- A locked door cannot become open merely because the next shot needs it; show the owner, tool, system response or force chain.
- Do not reset latch, hinge direction, opening angle, damage, light strip or who stands on each side between shots.

### Threshold Action-Match Transition

Use this when one bodily action crosses from one space, time, identity or reality state into another:

```text
source hand / foot / shoulder / gaze action
-> direction, speed and screen position become clear
-> handle, door edge, body or foreground cover reaches the edit point
-> hidden cut or controlled material change
-> target shot inherits the same action phase and vector
-> new light, material, sound or ownership difference appears after continuity is accepted
-> body completes the action and leaves a residual state
```

Useful variants:

- hand turns one handle and completes the turn on a different door.
- foot crosses a floor line and lands on a different material or time state.
- source door closes over frame; target door opens from the same edge and direction.
- character rounds a corridor corner; the body and camera vector continue while the environment changes.
- shoulder/body wipe hides the cut; the new space inherits screen position, gait phase and sound tail.
- stair landing, elevator door or curtain edge becomes the shared geometric boundary.

Preserve action phase, screen direction, body weight, contact hand/foot, opening edge and sound pulse. Reveal the new-world difference only after the viewer accepts the continuous action.

Failure boundary:

- Cutting before contact or after the action has fully stopped removes the match force.
- Opposite screen direction, different supporting foot, changed grip or unrelated camera height exposes the splice.
- A matched action with no information, permission, emotional or spatial change is only an editing demonstration.
- For AIGC, generate source and target sides as separately controlled plates when one prompt cannot preserve exact contact and environment change.

### Camera-Body Collision Chain

For wall contact, abrupt stops, doorway squeezes, stumbles, falls or obstacle impacts, design body and camera together:

```text
incoming force/direction
-> contact point and surface resistance
-> body compression / balance loss
-> camera response appropriate to the device
-> secondary cloth/hair/prop inertia
-> recovery step or final rest
-> residual mark and tail frame
```

The camera may react to the collision, but it keeps screen direction and obstacle geography readable. Use `dialogue-physical-interaction-grammar.md` for the full force/contact syntax and `counterpart-execution-risk-atlas.md` for twenty mechanism examples.

## 5. Axis And Dialogue Coverage

Dialogue scenes must not become flat face ping-pong. Axis, coverage, shot size, and lens choice describe power.

### 180-degree rule

- Establish the line of action between speakers before cutting singles.
- Keep screen direction consistent unless crossing the line is a deliberate disorientation or power break.
- If the axis is broken, motivate it through a character crossing the room, a camera move that visibly crosses, or a major emotional reversal.

### Basic coverage tools

- Establishing two-shot: locks geography, distance, table/door/window/object between characters.
- Over-shoulder: keeps opponent pressure in frame; shoulder size controls threat.
- Singles: isolate emotional state; shot size changes can shift power.
- Inserts: hands, phone, document, ring, glass, key, knife, pill, photo, clock; only use when the object changes the conversation.
- Reactions: listener often carries more truth than speaker.

### Power through coverage

- Wider shot = person is small inside relationship or room.
- Tighter shot = emotion, pressure, or dominance increases.
- Low angle = power or threat; high angle = vulnerability or surveillance.
- Long lens = compressed confrontation; wide lens close to face = subjective discomfort.
- A power reversal can be staged by slowly changing who gets the closer, lower, cleaner, or more stable frame.

### Typical dialogue cases

- Couple divorce fight: start with a two-shot separated by table/bed/doorway; use singles to show emotional split; inserts of ring, keys, cup, phone, or suitcase only when they trigger a line or choice; power can reverse when one character stands and the other remains seated.
- Interrogation: suspect can sit in shadow or light depending on information; interviewer may own the foreground, door, file, recorder, or lamp; reaction shots and hand inserts often matter more than accusations.
- Family dinner: keep table geography; use foreground bodies and plates to block alliances; off-screen kitchen or hallway sound can interrupt polite dialogue.
- Doctor/patient or authority dialogue: authority may own desk, light, screen, document, or doorway; patient is framed smaller or boxed by furniture unless the power reverses.

## 6. On-Site Lighting Execution Tools

Lighting execution asks what to add, subtract, block, bounce, motivate, or reveal.

| Tool | What it does | Use it when | Failure boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| softbox | large soft source, gentle shadow transition | memory, false safety, beauty, controlled domestic light | too frontal becomes flat and empty |
| reflector / bounce | returns light into shadow, keeps detail | preserve skin, reveal dark prop, soften contrast | over-bounce kills suspense and face volume |
| flag / black flag | blocks spill, shapes light, creates darkness | hide background, cut light from face, create sharp edge | random black patches look accidental |
| negative fill | removes bounce, deepens shadow side | make face sculptural, isolate person, raise contrast | can become dead black if no rim or detail remains |
| top light | overhead institutional pressure, fatigue | hospitals, offices, interrogation, elevators, kitchens | ugly without reason; hollow eyes need narrative purpose |
| practical light | visible motivated source | lamp, candle, monitor, phone, corridor tube, streetlight, appliance | if too weak or illogical, it cannot justify the look |
| window daylight / moonlight | directional motivated source | morning contrast, dusk split, cold night, outside pressure | flat window fill loses hierarchy |
| haze / volumetric light | reveals beams and air texture | dust, smoke, old rooms, stage, dream, ritual, sunlight path | decorative haze without source/path feels fake |

### Common lighting schemes

| Scheme | Base setup | Visual use | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rembrandt | side-front high key light, triangle cheek patch, controlled fill | secretive face, moral split, dialogue pressure | eye sockets must still read if emotion matters |
| butterfly | frontal high beauty light | glamour, public mask, performance | wrong for gritty suspense unless used ironically |
| side-back light | key or edge from side/rear, face partly hidden | mystery, separation, stalking, silhouette | add enough edge/detail to read body direction |
| flat light | broad frontal or overhead evenness | bureaucracy, dead normality, CCTV, fake safety | looks amateur unless emptiness is the point |
| skull top light | hard top light hollowing eyes | institutional depletion, interrogation, horror fatigue | overuse becomes ugly without meaning |
| window moonlight | cool side/back source, low exposure | night suspense, off-screen outside pressure | avoid pure blue cliche; preserve material texture |
| window morning light | warm/neutral low side source, softer contrast | new day, fragile safety, domestic irony | too beautiful can drain tension |
| volumetric / god light | hard directional source through haze | revelation, dust, ritual, abandoned space | beam needs motivated source and visible particles |
| screen light | blue/green/cyan practical from device | investigation, isolation, system, late-night proof | needs real screen position and flicker/level logic |
| candle / small lamp | `1800-3000K` small warm source, rapid falloff | intimate trap, ritual, fragile life, object focus | warm light must not automatically equal safety |

On-site lighting wording:

```text
practical source -> key placement -> fill or negative fill -> flags/bounce -> rim/separation -> background light -> color temperature -> exposure/shadow detail -> what changes during the beat
```

## 7. Quick Execution Checklist

Before finalizing a formal storyboard row, check:

- Camera position: height, angle, distance, subject placement, viewpoint owner.
- Lens: focal length family plus perspective/emotional reason.
- Focus: aperture/depth choice plus focus route when information changes.
- Movement: device, route, speed, end frame, and new information.
- Axis: dialogue line, screen direction, coverage plan, power shift.
- Lighting: source, add/subtract/block/bounce logic, color temperature, contrast, shadow share, readable detail.
- Shootability: can a crew or AIGC shot actually produce the camera path, focus change, axis, lighting source, and transition?
