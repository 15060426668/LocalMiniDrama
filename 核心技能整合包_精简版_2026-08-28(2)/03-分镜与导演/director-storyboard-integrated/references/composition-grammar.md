# Composition Grammar

Use this reference when a task needs composition design, frame layout, visual hierarchy, director-specific composition, or a stronger "what does the frame look like" answer. Composition is not a label. It is the visible placement of information in foreground, midground, background, frame edges, geometry, scale, and empty space.

## 1. Composition Core Law

Every composition must answer:

- What owns the frame: person, object, room, absence, screen, door, window, light, shadow, evidence, or geometry?
- What does the audience know from the frame that the character does not know?
- Which area is intentionally readable, blocked, empty, compressed, or delayed?
- How do foreground, midground, and background each carry information?
- What geometry controls the eye: one-point perspective, symmetry, repeated array, frame-within-frame, leading line, diagonal, vertical drop, mirror, screen, or negative space?
- How does scale change emotion: small person / large space, large object / trapped person, tiny clue / dominant silence?

Required writing order:

```text
foreground -> midground -> background -> subject placement -> frame edge / empty area -> geometry -> information function
```

## 2. Hitchcock Composition: Viewing Position And Information Gap

Core equation:

```text
composition = where the audience is allowed to look from + what the character cannot see yet
```

Use Hitchcock-type composition when suspense depends on viewing, spying, object anxiety, known danger, or delayed reveal.

### Reusable tools

- **Voyeur frame**: window, door gap, keyhole, curtain, blind, monitor, mirror, or camera lens sits in the foreground. The viewer watches through a barrier, so the act of looking becomes guilty.
- **Frame-within-frame**: every window, screen, doorway, hotel room, stairwell, or apartment rectangle becomes a smaller stage. Multiple small frames can show multiple lives or threats at once.
- **Known-danger layout**: place the danger/object in one readable part of the frame while the character remains unaware in another area. The viewer's anxiety comes from wanting the character to notice.
- **Spatial imprisonment**: trap the subject inside bars, stair rails, doorframes, window frames, narrow corridors, or furniture grids. The environment looks like a cage before the plot says so.
- **Reaction architecture**: compose around watcher -> watched object/person -> delayed reaction. The watched thing often matters less than the watcher realizing it.
- **Vertical fear / spiral fear**: stairs, wells, towers, overhead views, and spiral geometry visualize falling, vertigo, guilt, or loss of control.
- **Open-space threat**: an exposed field, road, lobby, or empty public space can be terrifying when there is no cover. Safety is denied by too much visibility.

### Visible frame patterns

- Foreground: dark room edge, curtain slit, window bar, lens rim, door crack, or partial shoulder.
- Midground: watcher, unaware character, or object carrier.
- Background: the danger, returning figure, suspicious window, stairwell, or empty space where the viewer expects proof.
- Geometry: frame-within-frame, sightline triangle, diagonal stair, vertical drop, or object-centered axis.
- Information function: audience sees the trap earlier than the character; the frame becomes a waiting device.

### Failure boundaries

- Do not let the audience know less than the character in a Hitchcock-style suspense beat.
- Do not use windows, blinds, or frames as decoration; they must define who is watching, who is watched, and what is withheld.
- Do not reveal the danger and resolve it in the same instant. The frame needs delay.
- Do not occlude so much that the audience cannot read the suspense carrier.
- Do not use dolly zoom, overhead view, or diagonal distortion unless the subjective fear or information shift needs it.

## 3. Nolan Composition: Geometry, Rule, Scale, And Physical Clarity

Core equation:

```text
composition = physical rule made visible through geometry + scale returned to human/object consequence
```

Use Nolan-type composition when a scene depends on rules, time, architecture, large scale, nested layers, physical impossibility, or rational awe.

### Reusable tools

- **One-point perspective**: corridors, runways, bridges, streets, rooms, and machines converge toward a single vanishing point. The frame tells the viewer the world has a rule.
- **Strict symmetry**: centered subjects, mirrored sides, repeated left/right structures, red/blue or forward/backward zones. Symmetry visualizes order, time reversal, ritual, or trap logic.
- **Geometric array**: repeated doors, columns, lockers, display cases, seats, windows, machines, or light panels. Repetition turns space into a system.
- **Scale hierarchy**: a huge object, building, wave, planet, vehicle, room, or device dominates the frame, but one human, hand, face, clock, or object anchors the emotional cost.
- **Physical threshold**: door, bridge, elevator, window, corridor end, airlock, rotating room, stair, or line on floor marks entry into a rule change.
- **Hard layer separation**: memory/real/time-space layers should be visually separated by architecture, color zones, light direction, or frame geometry rather than vague blending.
- **Recursive / maze composition**: mirror, corridor, nested room, repeating frame, or looped path turns time and memory into visible structure.

### Visible frame patterns

- Foreground: threshold object, rail, doorway, console, clock, hand, helmet, or small human anchor.
- Midground: rule space: corridor, machine, room grid, rotating structure, or repeated array.
- Background: vanishing point, impossible scale, horizon, nested frame, or second layer of time/space.
- Geometry: centered axis, one-point perspective, symmetry, grid, repeated modules, scale contrast.
- Information function: the viewer understands the rule before the sequence complicates it.

### Failure boundaries

- Do not use symmetry as pure beauty. It must teach a rule, pressure, ritual, or trap.
- Do not build scale without a human or object anchor; large images become empty spectacle.
- Do not fragment the frame before the geography is clear.
- Do not let a complex concept float as abstract VFX; anchor it to architecture, object, clock, body, or threshold.
- Do not overuse emotional close-ups when the emotional logic should be carried by space, light, object, or physical change.

## 4. Fincher Composition: System Control, Evidence, And Exact Placement

Core equation:

```text
composition = the system sees everything + evidence is placed with surgical precision
```

Use Fincher-type composition when a scene depends on investigation, procedure, institutional pressure, digital proof, marriage/identity as performance, or controlled dread.

### Reusable tools

- **System frame**: architecture, office grids, hallway lines, police rooms, screens, desks, files, glass, elevators, cubicles, and surveillance views overpower people.
- **Exact central placement**: a person, body, box, laptop, note, diary, phone, or file is placed with uncomfortable precision. The frame feels designed by an unseen system.
- **Evidence plane**: documents, computer screens, news footage, photos, diaries, crime-scene notes, and interface windows become flat readable planes inside the frame.
- **Cold two-dimensional control**: characters are held in clean frontal or slight high-angle frames, as if being measured rather than comforted.
- **Tiny figure / large system**: people become small inside architecture, rain, office light, courtrooms, bedrooms, corridors, or digital interfaces.
- **Perfect room, wrong meaning**: a tidy domestic space, symmetrical bedroom, clean living room, or carefully placed object can feel false because it is too arranged.
- **Slow reveal of evidence**: the composition lets the viewer approach proof gradually: room -> object -> text/detail -> reaction.

### Visible frame patterns

- Foreground: file edge, monitor bezel, glass reflection, table, doorframe, evidence bag, phone, notebook, or empty chair.
- Midground: investigator, suspect, spouse, body, or person being measured by the room.
- Background: institutional grid, shelves, rain window, office lights, screens, corridor, courtroom, or domestic perfection.
- Geometry: centered proof, strict horizontals/verticals, screen rectangles, nested frames, architectural grids.
- Information function: the room, evidence, or system feels stronger than the person.

### Failure boundaries

- Do not make precision decorative. Every centered object, screen, file, or grid must carry evidence, control, or performance.
- Do not hide evidence in darkness; Fincher-style darkness must preserve readable information.
- Do not use handheld chaos before the frame establishes system logic.
- Do not make every shot symmetrical; use symmetry when the scene needs control, falseness, or institutional pressure.
- Do not let screens and text become unreadable props. If the screen matters, its information hierarchy must be staged.

## 5. Transfer Templates

### Hitchcock: Watching The Unaware

- Foreground: door gap, curtain, window frame, monitor edge, or dark room side blocks 20%-40% of the frame.
- Midground: watcher or unaware subject occupies one side.
- Background: danger, returning person, suspicious object, or empty threshold sits in the opposite area.
- Geometry: frame-within-frame plus sightline triangle.
- Best use: a character is watched, a clue is visible, or the audience knows danger is approaching.

### Nolan: Rule Space

- Foreground: physical threshold, object, clock, hand, or small human anchor.
- Midground: one-point corridor, machine, grid, repeated array, or architectural system.
- Background: vanishing point, huge scale, second layer, or impossible physical rule.
- Geometry: strict symmetry, one-point perspective, repeated modules.
- Best use: rule setup, time/space logic, mission pressure, physical threshold, or large-scale consequence.

### Fincher: Evidence Under Control

- Foreground: file, phone, screen, desk edge, glass, evidence bag, or frame obstruction.
- Midground: character held still, observed, or measured by the room.
- Background: institutional grid, domestic order, rain window, screens, shelves, or corridor.
- Geometry: centered evidence plane, precise horizontals/verticals, nested rectangles.
- Best use: investigation, system pressure, false domestic normality, procedural reveal, or psychological control.

## 6. Failure Checklist

- The composition field says only "symmetry", "negative space", "frame-within-frame", or "Fincher look" without a drawable frame.
- Foreground, midground, and background do not each contain readable information.
- Geometry is pretty but not tied to rule, information, power, scale, or evidence.
- The frame has no owner: viewer cannot tell whether to watch person, object, room, screen, or absence.
- Empty space is not assigned a threat, expectation, or emotional function.
- The director grammar is used as a style name rather than a frame machine.
- AIGC short-drama frame holds longer than 4 seconds without new visual/audio information.
