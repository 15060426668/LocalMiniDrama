## Core Observation

AIGC short drama does not win by being merely fast. It wins by being dense:

- first 3 seconds: one visual question.
- first 15 seconds: one clear premise or danger.
- every 15 seconds: a new hook.
- every 30 seconds: a changed audience question.
- every 60 seconds: a stronger problem, not just a new background.

Film-level composition, lighting, and texture can remain. Film-level waiting time must be compressed.

## Pomegranate Trailer Contact Sheet Mechanism Deposit

Observed source: local contact sheet `pomegranate_trailer_storyboard_contact_sheet.jpg`, about 63 timestamped frames arranged as a trailer storyboard.

Why the image feels real and delicate:

- It uses imperfect human surfaces: age spots, pores, wet eyes, wrinkles, asymmetric expressions, thin hair, swollen skin, medical contact points.
- It gives every shot a physical surface: glass, water, metal tubing, cloth, mud, skin, red translucent lens, old monitors, cable clusters, palace fabric, field grass.
- Light looks motivated by practical sources: lab screens, cold skylight, colored reflection, underwater glow, exterior daylight, warm interior bulbs.
- The palette changes by story block but keeps controlled contrast: cold green-blue lab, red/orange glass-reflection, pale skin, warm gold dress, muted landscape.
- Backgrounds are not empty. They contain readable machinery, cables, bodies, water spray, landscape depth, or glass reflections.
- It alternates texture scale: face pores -> machine surface -> water volume -> landscape -> eye/lens macro -> title card.
- It avoids clean AI polish by using dirt, dampness, specular highlights, imperfect focus, shallow depth, and atmospheric haze.

Why the rhythm feels compact and powerful:

- The first 3 seconds already deliver three questions: who are these creatures, what attack/escape is happening, why is someone submerged in color and panic?
- The trailer does not cut randomly. It repeats anchors: old bald man, white-suited experiment subject, lab machinery, red eye/lens, water, female figure, title.
- Every 1-3 seconds changes shot type: grotesque close-up -> action wide -> water impact -> sensory close-up -> reaction face -> experiment room.
- It groups chaos into blocks: opening shock, lab explanation, body/sea scale, golden outdoor contrast, underwater electricity, eye/device proof, final confrontation, title.
- It uses reaction shots as glue. Repeated faces tell the viewer this is one story, even when the imagery is surreal.
- It withholds explanation but not orientation: viewers do not know the plot, but they know what to look at in each frame.
- Title cards arrive only after enough image memory has accumulated, so the title feels like release, not interruption.

Transfer template:

| Time | Trailer Function | Shot Type Mix |
|---:|---|---|
| 0-3s | violent visual question | impossible face/action/impact |
| 3-8s | human anchor | face close-up + environment proof |
| 8-15s | rule/system hint | repeated character + machine/object |
| 15-30s | scale expansion | body pressure + wide surreal space + reaction |
| 30-45s | emotional contrast | warm/beautiful image interrupted by danger |
| 45-60s | evidence climax | eye/object/device macro + confrontation close-ups |
| final | memory stamp | title card after visual overload |

## AIGC Short-Drama Rhythm Template

| Time | Script Job | Storyboard Job | Sound Job |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 0-3s | visual question | one image that asks "what is happening?" | one sound that enters before explanation |
| 3-8s | playable objective | show what the character is doing to survive, escape, hide, test, or choose | near foley and breath lock viewer to body |
| 8-15s | first hook | reveal rule/anomaly/object/speaker/space change | off-screen or internal voice adds pressure |
| 15-30s | first turn | safety becomes trap, exit changes, object reacts, sound lies | sound bridge or silence marks the turn |
| 30-60s | premise lock | define main task, cost, and character weakness | repeat one motif with changed meaning |
| 60s+ | escalation rhythm | every minute tightens rule, space, relationship, or time | each escalation has a sound signature |

## Trailer Rhythm Training Protocol

Treat trailers as rhythm evidence, not as scene-continuity owners. Separate two levels:

- macro wave: slow burn, acceleration, silence break, impact block, emotional contrast, evidence climax, final sting.
- micro cut curve: shot-length changes, beat synchronization, reaction inserts, repeated anchors and title-card spacing.

For every trailer sample, record:

```text
opening question / rhythm blocks / approximate shot-length curve
/ recurring face-object-space anchor / sound motif and silence point
/ information revealed per block / title-card function / final sting
/ transferable short-drama rule / failure boundary
```

Five useful waveforms:

1. slow accumulation -> one rupture: dread and premium suspense.
2. steady acceleration -> impact climax: chase, countdown and system failure.
3. fast block -> silence -> single image: cognitive shock and rule reveal.
4. beauty/warmth -> interruption -> danger block: emotional contrast.
5. repeated motif with changed meaning: object, phrase, sound or face returns as the hook.

Retiming rule: preserve the waveform and anchor recurrence, then rebuild every cut around the current short's causality. A trailer may teach when to accelerate or withhold; it does not supply the short's shot order.

Failure boundaries:

- Random one-second images without a repeated anchor feel like a showreel.
- Music-only editing collapses when dialogue, foley or silence enters.
- Title cards that explain missing story pressure interrupt retention.
- A fast block still needs screen direction, identity and one active audience question.

## Practical Rules

- Start late. Enter the scene at the moment the body is already under pressure.
- Do not spend the first shot "establishing atmosphere"; establish a problem.
- Use one continuity anchor through quick cuts: the same door, flame, rule voice, bag strap, foot direction, or color light.
- A 15-second segment may contain 3-5 visual changes, but no more than one main objective.
- If using a long move, embed micro-beats: new foreground, new background proof, new light behavior, new sound trigger, new objective.
- Keep exposition as action pressure. A rule should immediately make the character almost do the wrong thing.
- Preserve one emotional reason to continue: fear for self, fear for someone loved, shame, guilt, mission, or a rule that touches a weakness.

## Failure Boundaries

- No animated PPT: no static faces plus narration as the main engine.
- No lore dump in the first 15 seconds.
- No empty fast cutting; every cut must answer or delay a question.
- No transformation soup; each VFX change must carry story information.
- No long static hold over 4 seconds unless something changes inside the frame.
- No visual quality replacing drama. Filmic texture is surface; conflict is engine.

## Rule-Corridor Opening Example

Recommended rhythm:

| Time | Beat |
|---:|---|
| 0-3s | fixed off-axis corridor frame: the subject runs in from darkness and the visual question starts immediately |
| 3-8s | low foot-follow: body panic, wardrobe inertia and passing corridor lights establish speed and direction |
| 8-15s | a self-anchor routine enters; the first doorframe wipe mutates one material family |
| 15-30s | the route becomes unstable through two or three distinct threshold changes; the target door remains the only stable objective |
| 30-45s | the subject reaches and opens the threshold; the transition lands in a new enclosed space with bodily residue intact |

Execution locks:

- keep one objective and one direction readable through every mutation.
- introduce rule objects only when the story phase requires them.
- protect later reveals from premature faces, symbols or exposition.
- carry body, wardrobe, dirt/wetness, sound and camera direction into the landing space.
- decide whether the route is a loop, residue, reset or spatial lie; do not imply all four.
