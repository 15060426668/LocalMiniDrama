## 1. Visual Thesis

The house style combines emotional accessibility with material seriousness:

```text
soft youthful bodies and simple readable faces
+ thin human linework and restrained cel color
+ whole-body acting and clear hand/object contact
+ materially richer ordinary environments
+ bright or warm light that can coexist with pain
+ local roughness only where psychology or force requires it
```

The image should feel drawn, observed, and lived in. It is neither glossy generic anime nor loose unfinished sketching. The defining contrast is clean character readability against backgrounds with more material memory, while ordinary daylight refuses to automatically announce whether a moment is safe.

## 2. Stable Family Identity

Keep these constants across every project, genre, period, and country:

- youthful silhouettes with slightly oversized heads, narrow shoulders, slim limbs, and soft joint transitions;
- short, soft jawlines rather than pointed V-shaped chins;
- sparse facial marks with a small nose and compact mouth;
- large coherent irises, restrained sclera, and eyelids that carry most emotional nuance;
- hair built as broad graphic masses with a few irregular edge strands;
- thin charcoal or dark-brown contours with visible but modest width variation;
- matte skin and cloth, one restrained cel-shadow family, sparse highlights;
- cheek hatching and under-eye texture used locally rather than airbrushed blush;
- folds placed at force, bend, weight, and contact points rather than covering every garment;
- character paint cleaner than background paint;
- simple high-chroma mascot forms with much lower detail density than the human world.

Age, nationality, costume, body build, and genre can vary without abandoning this family identity.

## 3. Character Construction

### 3.1 Age-relative proportions

Do not make every character equally chibi.

- Young child: visibly larger head, short torso, soft shoulders, small hands and feet, compact reach.
- Older child or young teen: compact head-to-body ratio, narrow ribcage, slim limbs, slightly long forearms and calves, ordinary awkward posture.
- Older teen: retain the soft face family while lengthening neck, torso, hands, and legs; keep shoulders natural rather than heroic.
- Adult: use adult height, shoulder breadth, hand size, facial plane, posture, and clothing weight; preserve sparse facial construction and the same line/cel system.
- Stylized mascot: round or bean-like body, minimal appendages, clear squashable silhouette, tiny face marks.

Body diversity is expressed through silhouette, shoulder/hip relation, limb thickness, posture, and clothing fit. Do not replace age or body type with a single face template.

### 3.2 Face shape

- Use a broad upper cranium and soft taper toward a short chin.
- Keep cheeks gently full in children and narrower but still soft in older characters.
- Let the face be slightly asymmetrical: one eyelid higher, one cheek line longer, one mouth corner more active.
- Keep the nose minimal: a dot, short soft stroke, tiny wedge, or low-contrast shadow depending on angle.
- Define ears simply but place them anatomically relative to eyes and jaw.
- Leave open skin areas. Emotional density should not fill the entire face with marks.

### 3.3 Eyes

Eyes are the main recognition anchor, but their size and gloss change by emotional lane.

Baseline eye:

- large dark or colored iris occupying much of the eye opening;
- restrained white sclera;
- upper eyelid slightly stronger than the lower lid;
- one main catchlight and one small supporting reflection;
- simple lower-lid line or no lower line in ordinary scenes;
- lashes grouped into a few readable points, not a dense cosmetic fan.

Quiet emotion:

- lower the upper lid, reduce highlight area, let gaze sit slightly off target;
- use a faint lower-lid shadow and one or two short under-eye strokes;
- keep the iris coherent rather than filling it with photographic reflections.

Wonder or joy:

- open the eye vertically, allow a warm secondary reflection or graphic sparkle;
- keep highlight shapes simple and emotionally owned;
- let brows, neck extension, shoulders, and hands support the eye expression.

Pressure or fatigue:

- flatten the eye opening, deepen the hair cast shadow or lower-lid tone, shift focus away;
- preserve pale skin midtones so tiredness remains readable;
- use local hatching rather than turning the whole face grey.

Closed-eye smiles use long soft arcs, not rigid upside-down crescents. The cheeks, mouth, shoulders, and body lean must complete the smile.

### 3.4 Brows, nose, mouth, cheeks

- Brows are thin, simple, and mobile. Their distance from the eyelid matters more than texture.
- The nose stays subordinate to eyes and mouth.
- A quiet mouth is a short line, tiny curve, small parted wedge, or compact oval.
- A joyful open mouth becomes one bold graphic shape with a simple tongue or inner warm plane.
- Teeth appear as one clean light shape when needed, not individually rendered.
- Cheek color is pale peach or muted pink, often reinforced by short vertical or diagonal hand-drawn strokes.
- Blush marks follow cheek volume and emotional tension; they remain sparse and uneven.

### 3.5 Hair

Construct hair from silhouette before strands:

1. lock the outer head-and-hair mass;
2. divide it into a few broad directional clumps;
3. add sparse interior separation lines only where clumps overlap;
4. add irregular edge wisps to reveal wind, fatigue, speed, or lack of grooming;
5. reserve highlights as broad low-contrast shapes or a few clean strips.

Black hair should read as a near-solid charcoal mass with enough local lift to separate it from dark backgrounds. Blonde or colored hair uses broad flat planes and a limited warm/cool shadow family. Hair must not dissolve into thousands of photoreal strands.

### 3.6 Hands and feet

Hands are narrative instruments, not decorative appendages.

- Use simplified tapered fingers with visible knuckle direction and a clear thumb base.
- Preserve the action: grip, hover, press, splay, cradle, point, pull, or release.
- Show contact compression where fingers press cloth, glass, a strap, paper, another hand, or a prop.
- Keep the wrist angle and forearm route consistent with the action.
- In close foreshortening, enlarge the near hand while keeping palm/finger structure readable.
- Feet and shoes show body route, support, hesitation, speed, or collapse through toe direction and heel state.

### 3.7 Clothing

- Prefer ordinary lived-in garments: loose T-shirts, school uniforms, hoodies, shorts, skirts, cardigans, backpacks, simple workwear, or culturally specific daily clothes.
- Use broad flat color regions and a small number of folds.
- Place folds at shoulders, armpits, elbows, waist compression, lap, knees, straps, hand grips, and fabric weight.
- Oversized shirts hang from shoulders and gather at contact points rather than clinging to the torso.
- Add wear, chalk, dust, sweat, wrinkles, or frayed edges only when story and environment require them.
- Keep costume design culturally precise without changing the house drawing language.

### 3.8 Mascot or impossible companion

- Build a clean round or bean-like silhouette with tiny eyes and mouth.
- Use one high-chroma flat color, usually clearly separated from human skin and clothing.
- Keep surface detail minimal; emotion comes from overall tilt, squash, stretch, appendage direction, and distance to humans.
- Let the human environment remain richer and more material so the mascot feels visually foreign.
- Graphic sparkles, hearts, stars, ribbons, or simple flowers may belong to the mascot's interpretation of a moment, not to the objective baseline.

## 4. Linework System

### 4.1 Baseline contour

Use a fine charcoal or warm dark-brown contour. Keep it darker than local color but softer than mechanical vector black. Allow small pressure changes, slightly imperfect corners, and occasional tapered ends.

Line hierarchy:

- outer silhouette: clearest and slightly stronger;
- overlap/contact line: clear enough to establish depth and ownership;
- facial line: lighter and more economical;
- hair interior line: sparse, directional, subordinate to the mass;
- clothing fold: only where force or material changes direction;
- psychological hatch: thinnest, shortest, and locally clustered;
- background line: softer, broken, or partially absorbed into paint with distance.

### 4.2 Clean key-art line

- crisp silhouette with gentle human variation;
- open faces and clean garment fields;
- minimal background or white/cream graphic field;
- sparse decorative marks that support the current emotion;
- promotional clarity without vector sterility.

### 4.3 Broadcast-frame line

- slightly softer contour and lower micro-detail;
- broad consistent color fields;
- small capture grain and mild edge softness;
- facial marks simplified at wider scales;
- background paint carries depth while characters remain readable.

### 4.4 Rough emotional line

Roughness is local and motivated:

- short pencil hatching beneath eyes, along cheeks, neck, hands, or hair shadow;
- one or two broken contour sections around hair, clothing, or trembling hands;
- denser marks in shadow masses, not over pale skin everywhere;
- stronger directional strokes only at a force peak or graphic illustration lane.

Do not make every contour scratchy. A clean baseline is required for roughness to have meaning.

## 5. Cel Shading and Light

### 5.1 Baseline cel model

- pale matte skin midtone;
- one main shadow family with clean designed shapes;
- limited soft contamination from sky, wall, foliage, or warm interior light;
- small graphic highlights on eyes, hair, glass, or a key prop;
- broad light logic rather than airbrushed form rendering.

Shadow shapes should explain planes and relationships: hair casting across a face, chin onto neck, sleeve over arm, body over bedding, object over floor, foreground over a smaller character.

### 5.2 Ordinary daylight

- sky or window acts as a broad source;
- faces stay open and pale;
- outdoor shadows lean cool grey-green or blue-grey;
- foliage and walls contribute reflected color;
- painful events may remain in bright, attractive surroundings.

### 5.3 Warm domestic or sunset light

- use peach, amber, ochre, or warm cream on lit planes;
- retain neutral or muted cool shadow separation;
- keep the warmth physically motivated by sun, wall bounce, lamp, or interior spill;
- do not assume warm light means emotional safety.

### 5.4 Psychological localization

Keep the environment credible and localize pressure through:

- hair shadow crossing eyes;
- grey-olive or grey-violet under-eye tone;
- darker neck, collar, or hand contact;
- foreground occlusion;
- compressed light access around the subject;
- one distant saturated accent tied to the emotional cause.

### 5.5 Bright emotional peak

Increase eye reflections, warm light, graphic sparkles, or simplified background only when the character or mascot owns that interpretation. Keep at least one reality anchor such as clothing weight, hand contact, glass reflection, windblown hair, or a real environment edge.

## 6. Color System

Assign every palette by role rather than applying one global tint:

```text
skin midtone
hair mass
clothing blocks
environment local colors
motivated light color
shadow bias
one small narrative accent
```

### 6.1 Ordinary outdoor palette

- pale warm ivory skin with light peach cheeks;
- charcoal black, warm brown, or clear ochre-blonde hair;
- cyan to turquoise sky;
- layered bottle green, moss, and yellow-green foliage;
- cool concrete grey or dusty earth;
- one restrained coral, red, lavender, teal, or yellow prop accent.

### 6.2 School and home palette

- faded teal or green frames;
- dusty salmon, cream, off-white, muted wood, grey-green shadow;
- ordinary paper, fabric, plastic, glass, and painted metal colors;
- one clean high-chroma mascot or personal object.

### 6.3 Lived-in interior palette

- muted brown, grey, olive, cream, faded red, washed navy, and dull plastic color;
- pale bedding or skin as the visual island;
- clutter differentiated by material and value rather than equal saturation;
- selected cyan, coral, pink, or red objects guiding the eye.

### 6.4 Emotional close-up palette

- preserve pale face mids;
- locally deepen brown-grey, olive-grey, blue-grey, or grey-violet shadows;
- keep hair and background separated by value or edge light;
- use warmth or saturation only where gaze, tears, blush, or a relationship cue requires it.

## 7. Surface and Capture Texture

The desired finish is matte, lightly tactile, and animation-native.

### Character surfaces

- clean stable cel-color regions;
- faint fine paper grain or animation-photography noise;
- soft edge contamination near bright windows or sky;
- small irregular blush and under-eye strokes;
- matte skin with minimal specular shine;
- hair highlights grouped into broad shapes.

### Background surfaces

- denser paint and material information than the characters;
- clustered foliage masses with broken leaf edges;
- wood grain, tatami weave, bedding wrinkles, paper edges, plastic scuffs, metal dullness, floor seams, wall wear, dust, and ordinary clutter;
- depth created by value grouping, overlap, atmospheric softness, and changing detail density;
- slight bloom only around strong sky, window, reflective glass, or emotional light.

### Capture finish

- light animation-frame grain;
- mild tonal softness rather than aggressive sharpening;
- coherent color fields without banded 3D gradients;
- crisp eyes and contact points, softer distant paint;
- promotional art may be cleaner, while broadcast stills may retain a little optical softness.

## 8. Environment Design

Backgrounds are ordinary worlds with material memory.

- Build at least three depth planes when the scene is not a flat graphic field.
- Include culturally and functionally correct objects, not decorative filler.
- Group clutter into readable islands and paths; do not distribute detail evenly.
- Let one large simple value shape protect the subject, such as bedding, sky, wall, desk surface, road, or window light.
- Use architecture and objects to express scale, access, belonging, or pressure.
- Keep daily routines visible through open books, bags, dishes, laundry, floor wear, chairs, shoes, posters, containers, or work objects.
- When the environment dominates, make the figure smaller but keep silhouette, skin value, and pose legible.

### Overhead lived-in frame

Use a near-vertical top view, one light bedding or floor island, a centered or slightly offset body, and object clusters around the perimeter. Vary object scale and orientation; connect each item to a believable life task. Keep the figure calm and simply rendered so the room tells history around them.

### Forest or outdoor frame

Use large dark trunks or foliage masses, mid-value ground, bright sky openings, and dappled edge light. Characters remain simple, lower in detail, and clearly separated from the background by value and silhouette.

### School or institutional frame

Use desks, doors, corridor panels, windows, bags, papers, shoes, and repeated structural lines to establish social geography. The environment can enclose, isolate, or connect without changing to a horror palette.

## 9. Static Composition Grammar

Choose one dominant grammar per image.

### Ensemble key art

- stagger head heights and body directions;
- give each character one distinct gesture, facial state, and prop relationship;
- use overlaps to imply friendship or tension without hiding silhouettes;
- keep a clean field or simple background so line and personality dominate.

### Intimate two- or three-person frame

- use knees, hands, paper, bags, shoulders, or shared objects to connect the group;
- vary gaze routes rather than making everyone face the viewer;
- preserve small asymmetries and quiet space between bodies.

### Environmental dominance

- let room, forest, road, classroom, or sky occupy most of the frame;
- make the person a clear small shape inside a larger ordinary world;
- use light, bedding, ground, or a geometric opening as the subject island.

### Psychological close-up

- crop tightly enough that eyelids, mouth, cheek marks, hair shadow, and one hand/prop cue matter;
- compress or simplify the background;
- keep one side of the frame open or occluded to express attention and pressure;
- use local texture rather than global darkness.

### Child-height or low-angle frame

- allow foreground objects or adult bodies to enlarge naturally;
- preserve readable ground/support and hand routes;
- use the larger foreground to express access, curiosity, or power without fisheye spectacle.

### Group-pressure frame

- arrange people and objects as a U-shape, corridor, closed loop, or blocked exit;
- keep the vulnerable figure lower, smaller, partly occluded, or outside the main gaze circuit;
- give every visible person a distinct gaze or task state.

## 10. Rendering Lanes

### `CLEAN-KEY-ART`

Fine clean contours, flat pale colors, sparse cel shadow, crisp silhouette, expressive gestures, white/cream or simple graphic background, limited handwritten-style decorative marks.

### `BROADCAST-ORDINARY`

Soft stable contour, compact proportions, ordinary architecture or landscape, restrained one-step cel shadows, matte surfaces, faint capture grain, natural daylight.

### `LIVED-IN-DENSE`

Simple character paint inside a materially dense room or environment, organized clutter, fabric/wood/paper/plastic texture, muted earth palette, one light subject island.

### `INTIMATE-EMOTION`

Large readable face, wet iris or closed-eye arc, small nose and mouth, vertical cheek hatching, hair shadow, clean skin mids, one relationship or contact cue.

### `BRIGHT-PEAK`

Wider eye opening or bold graphic mouth, warm reflected light, limited sparkle/heart/star accents, clean silhouette, whole-body or neck/shoulder support, visible reality anchor.

### `PSYCHOLOGICAL-PRESSURE`

Heavy eyelids, off-target gaze, compressed shoulders, sparse under-eye hatching, localized olive/grey-violet shadow, ordinary daylight or room color retained, one pressure object or occlusion.

Select one primary lane. A supporting lane may affect only one local region, such as a graphic background behind an emotional face or psychological hatching beneath otherwise ordinary cel rendering.

## 11. Chinese/Japanese Culture Adapter

The house style remains stable; the cultural world changes.

### Chinese-animation setting

Specify the relevant Chinese region, period, school/home/public-space type, furniture, uniforms or daily clothes, food packaging, street signs, apartment materials, transport, landscape, and social posture. Use simplified Chinese only when exact readable text is necessary and feasible; otherwise use recognizable sign blocks without invented pseudo-writing.

### Japanese-animation setting

Specify the relevant Japanese region, period, school/home/public-space type, uniforms or casual clothes, genkan/tatami/flooring only when the location actually has them, local bags, stationery, road edges, railings, shop objects, and social posture. Do not import these markers into a Chinese setting by default.

### Historical or fantasy setting

Translate the same shape, line, cel, and performance system into culturally researched garments, architecture, tools, and materials. Do not turn the house style into ornate generic fantasy rendering.

### Shared rule

Nationality is never expressed by changing eye size or reducing a person to stereotype. Use identity references, local material culture, clothing construction, environment, and behavior.

## 12. Continuity Checksum

For a series, record:

```text
age and body silhouette
head/jaw/cheek shape
iris size, color, catchlight pattern, eyelid weight
nose and mouth grammar
hair outer mass, major clumps, edge wisps, highlight shape
skin midtone and cheek-hatch color
baseline contour color and hierarchy
baseline cel-shadow hue and hardness
clothing cut, flat-color blocks, fold density, wear state
hand/foot simplification standard
background-to-character detail ratio
paper/capture grain level
culture layer
allowed emotional lane deviations
```

Lock the checksum before producing a batch of character or scene images.

## 13. Failure Boundaries

Reject and rewrite when:

- the prompt depends on a title, artist, studio, or source name;
- faces use pointed chins, dense makeup lashes, tiny irises, or glossy 3D skin by default;
- hair becomes thousands of individual strands or plastic sculpted volume;
- smooth gradients replace graphic cel-shadow shapes;
- all contours have identical vector width;
- hatching and grain cover every surface equally;
- emotional scenes become globally dark instead of locally pressured;
- characters and backgrounds have equal micro-detail density;
- clothing has random decorative folds unrelated to gravity or contact;
- hands are hidden despite carrying the dramatic action;
- every scene uses hearts, stars, pink graphics, or giant wet eyes;
- Chinese and Japanese settings differ only by labels while props and architecture drift;
- a still prompt contains a timeline, camera move, transition, or multiple incompatible moments;
- Midjourney parameters appear anywhere in the final description.
