# Static Cinematic Foundations

Build one decisive still frame rather than a compressed video sequence.

## 1. Freeze one story moment

Choose a moment with visible before-and-after pressure:

- someone has just noticed another person;
- a route has just been blocked;
- a hand has just taken ownership of an object;
- a smile has just become socially threatening;
- a child is about to speak or has just chosen silence;
- a simple mascot has entered an ordinary human space.

Keep one primary action and one readable consequence. Put earlier or later events into posture, object state, gaze, or environmental residue.

## 2. Map the social space

For multi-person scenes, define:

- who owns the center;
- who owns the exit or route;
- who is smallest or most occluded;
- who looks at whom;
- which shoulders, bags, desks, doors, walls, or railings form the enclosure;
- which background person watches, laughs, hesitates, or continues an ordinary task.

Every person needs a distinct spatial job. Random crowd placement weakens social pressure.

## 3. Compose three layers

- Foreground: shoulder, school bag, desk edge, doorframe, pipe, grass, mascot, or cropped body fragment.
- Midground: the primary relationship or action.
- Background: ordinary school, home, road, trees, sky, or classmates continuing daily life.

Use environment-led depth for group scenes. Use simplified depth for psychological close-ups.

## 4. Choose perspective by meaning

- Wide close perspective makes bodies and bags feel physically present and can turn a group into a wall.
- Normal perspective keeps conversation and relationship distance believable.
- Long-lens compression isolates a face or makes a corridor feel socially closed.
- Mild tilt or edge stretch signals subjective discomfort only when the center remains readable.

Static prompts may describe camera position, framing, lens impression, depth, and focus. They do not describe a camera route through time.

## 5. Separate character and environment detail

- Ordinary faces use fewer internal lines than walls, trees, desks, clothing folds, and bags.
- Psychological pressure adds line density locally around eyes, cheeks, neck, hands, or hair shadow.
- Backgrounds may be painterly or textured while character fills stay clean and graphic.
- Preserve one dominant silhouette per character, especially black hair and rounded mascot forms.

## 6. Build emotional light from ordinary sources

Keep the source plausible: open sky, window, corridor daylight, pale wall bounce, classroom fluorescent, or forest canopy.

Pressure comes from:

- who remains in open light;
- who falls under hair, wall, doorway, or crowd shadow;
- how much of the eyes stay readable;
- whether the background remains cheerfully bright while one face darkens.

## 7. Static-frame gate

Reject and rebuild when:

- the prompt describes several seconds of sequential action;
- group count or identity roles are ambiguous;
- every child has the same pose and expression;
- the scene relies only on an emotion adjective;
- linework, shading, palette, and texture are unnamed;
- the background is generic and carries no social or spatial function;
- a named source substitutes for visible style evidence.
